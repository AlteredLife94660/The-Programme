# Contribution and power

## Contribution as a social concept

The source files propose recognizing actions that reduce harm, meet needs, strengthen stability, or build useful knowledge. This can help describe socially valuable activity that markets often overlook, including care, maintenance, learning, community work, and creative activity.

Contribution is context-dependent and hard to attribute. Outcomes can have many causes; useful work may be invisible, delayed, collective, or difficult to quantify. Paid work, public visibility, data availability, and model legibility are not reliable measures of a person's value. Unpaid care and necessary rest must not be treated as failure.

## Recognition without a human ranking

Any contribution measure should be voluntary where possible, limited to a clearly stated purpose, time-bounded, transparent, contestable, and independently assessed. It must never determine basic needs, legal rights, personal dignity, or access to due process. Do not create a universal social score or use a score to rank citizens, allocate coercive authority, or infer moral character.

If recognition or additional resources are considered, test whether the mechanism entrenches privilege, rewards easily measured work, disadvantages disabled or marginalized people, encourages gaming, or creates pressure to perform. Prefer supporting worthwhile activity directly over scoring individuals.

## Power is broader than wealth

Power can arise from control over money, essential resources, infrastructure, information, data, attention, organizational decisions, or algorithmic systems. It should be assessed by its real ability to constrain others and evade accountability, not merely by a single numeric proxy.

The source proposal for automatic decay captures a concern about entrenched advantage. A universal decay rate is not a neutral solution: it may undermine security or long-term projects and may fail to address power that is hard to measure. Consider plural checks—transparency, competition, anti-monopoly rules, separation of powers, term limits, collective bargaining, privacy protections, and independent oversight—through legitimate public processes.

## Guardrails for any future mechanism

- Preserve guaranteed access to essentials regardless of contribution or wealth.
- Do not use opaque prediction or inferred contribution to justify unequal rights.
- Make policy-level rules public, explainable, and open to challenge.
- Separate assessment from enforcement; no single agent or institution should control both.
- Audit effects on different groups and on people whose work is not easily measured.
- Include appeal, correction, and deletion mechanisms for inaccurate personal data.
- Set limits on collection, retention, access, and secondary use of data.
- Review whether the mechanism actually reduces domination and harm; sunset it if not.

Rewarding contribution may be useful, but it is subordinate to dignity, basic needs, fairness, and accountability.
