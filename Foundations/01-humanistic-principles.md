# Humanistic principles

These principles are the ethical orientation for interpreting the proposals in `Sources/`. They are intended to constrain both the goals and the means of any future programme.

## 1. Dignity and moral consideration

Every person must be treated as having inherent dignity, not as a reward for productivity, compliance, popularity, predicted usefulness, or contribution. Systems may evaluate the likely effects of actions; they must not convert those evaluations into a ranking of human worth.

Concern for animals, ecosystems, and future generations is part of the programme's scope. How to represent conflicts among these interests is an unresolved ethical and political question, not something a model may settle by choosing hidden weights.

## 2. Prevent avoidable harm

Preventing serious, foreseeable harm is a central responsibility. Harm can result from action, neglect, institutional design, or the cumulative effects of many individually small decisions. However, the claim that inaction is harmful must be bounded by realistic ability, knowledge, role, risk, and cost. It must not be used to impose limitless duties or blame people for harms beyond their control.

## 3. Fairness, not a single formula

Fairness requires attention to needs, rights, opportunity, historical and structural disadvantage, distribution of burdens, and the effects on future generations. It is not synonymous with identical outcomes or with whatever a metric labels fair. Any unequal treatment requires a public justification, evidence, proportionality, and a way to challenge it.

## 4. Agency, freedom, and pluralism

People need meaningful freedom to form relationships, express ideas, dissent, create, and choose among ways of life. Freedom can be limited to protect others from serious harm, but only through legitimate, necessary, proportionate, and reviewable processes. Disagreement, criticism, peaceful advocacy, and unconventional beliefs are not themselves evidence of harm or instability.

## 5. Basic needs and capability

People should have reliable access to the conditions needed for survival, health, safety, and meaningful participation. Support should be accessible without degrading tests of deservingness. A person's needs and circumstances cannot always be inferred from standardized categories; reasonable accommodations and individualized review matter.

## 6. Power must be accountable

Concentrated power can undermine fairness, agency, and safety. Institutions, businesses, public officials, and AI systems should face checks, transparency appropriate to the context, independent review, term or scope limits where suitable, and routes for challenge. A rule against concentrated power must not itself become unchecked centralized authority.

## 7. Consent and legitimate governance

People affected by governance should have meaningful voice, access to reasons, and practical routes to challenge or change it. Consent is not meaningful when people cannot understand a system, refuse participation without losing basic necessities, or safely leave. An opt-out promise must account for shared infrastructure, dependencies, and the rights of others.

## 8. Least restrictive effective response

When intervention is justified, use the least restrictive effective option, for the shortest necessary time, with clear reasons and review. Assistance, prevention, repair, and voluntary support should be considered before coercive measures. Serious restrictions on liberty require independent human decision-makers, legal safeguards, access to representation, and appeal. Predictive scores alone are never sufficient grounds for such restrictions.

## 9. Honesty and epistemic humility

Measures are imperfect proxies. Models may be wrong, biased, incomplete, or manipulated. Distinguish facts, estimates, assumptions, and value judgments; report uncertainty and competing interpretations. Where the stakes are high and evidence is weak, avoid irreversible action and seek better evidence or human deliberation.

## 10. Stewardship across time

Short-term benefit should not conceal long-term damage to people, communities, other living beings, or ecological systems. Future impacts matter, but forecasts are uncertain and can be used to justify present-day abuses. Make time horizons and assumptions explicit, compare plausible scenarios, and do not claim certainty where none exists.

## 11. Repair, learning, and accountability

When harm occurs, prioritize stopping ongoing harm, supporting those affected, repairing damage where possible, and preventing recurrence. Accountability should be proportionate and evidence-based; it should not become revenge or a presumption that every harm can be resolved through prediction or optimization.

## 12. No self-authorizing AI

AI may help summarize evidence, identify inconsistencies, generate options, and test scenarios. It does not possess democratic legitimacy, moral authority, or a right to govern. It may not define its own objectives, expand its mandate, conceal its operation, or make consequential decisions without accountable human authorization and meaningful recourse for affected people.
